## Overview

Most prioritization frameworks fail in opposite directions. RICE — Reach, Impact, Confidence, Effort — produces a clean number, but the number is only as good as the four guesses that produced it, and most teams are guessing without scaffolding. Pure AI prioritization tools, conversely, produce an answer with no defensibility — a recommendation the team cannot interrogate or revise.

Tarazu sits between the two. It runs RICE as the structural backbone — each dimension a normalized 1–100 input with the score computed in real time — and layers AI in two places where it earns its keep: grounded score *suggestions* the team can accept or override, and a one-click strategy read across the whole backlog. The framework stays a thinking aid, not a calculator, and the score stays the team's.

## Problem framing

Three observations shape the design space:

1. **The hardest part of RICE is the inputs, not the math.** Multiplying four numbers and dividing by effort is trivial. Choosing those numbers honestly — without anchoring to whatever number was used last quarter — is the actual work.
2. **Confidence is systematically overstated.** Teams run RICE workshops where every initiative is rated 80% confidence by default. The dimension that should reduce hubris ends up reinforcing it.
3. **Prioritization tools fail when they hide judgment.** A score that arrives without showing how it was assembled cannot be defended in a roadmap meeting and will not survive contact with engineering. An AI that *generates* the score outright reintroduces exactly that problem.

The opportunity: a tool that structures each RICE dimension, offers a defensible starting point where it can, and then shows the trade-offs across the whole backlog — without ever taking the judgment away from the team.

## Solution

Four design decisions defined the build:

### Normalized RICE, scored in real time

Each of the four dimensions is a 1–100 slider, and the RICE score recomputes as you move it. Normalizing to a common scale trades RICE's native units (reach counts, person-months) for comparability — every candidate is scored on the same axes, so the matrix and the ranking are apples-to-apples.

### AI suggests, the team decides

For each candidate, an AI pass (Claude Sonnet) proposes per-dimension scores grounded in the product context you give it. The suggestion is a starting point, never the answer — you accept, edit, or override every value, and the score belongs to the team. This is the deliberate line the build holds: AI that *generates a final score* from a pasted description was rejected, because it skips the discipline RICE exists to enforce; AI that *suggests and explains* keeps the human in the seat.

### The Priority Matrix

An Effort × Impact scatter, rendered on a 2D canvas, places the whole backlog into four labeled quadrants — Quick Win, Strategic, Fill-In, Avoid. The single score answers "what's first"; the matrix answers "what kind of bet is this," which is the question a roadmap conversation actually turns on.

### AI Strategy Advisor, separate from the score

One click runs Claude Opus across the scored backlog and returns a structured read: the top priority, the quick win, a risk flag, a suggested sprint plan, and a strategic insight. It runs independently from the RICE math, so the numbers stay auditable and the narrative stays a second opinion rather than the source of truth. A demo mode returns a representative read when no live model call is made.

## Implementation considerations

The original temptation — paste a feature description, get a finished RICE score — was rejected, and that decision still holds: an auto-generated score skips the discipline of making the team's assumptions explicit and reintroduces the very problem the tool exists to solve. What shipped instead is the narrower, defensible version of AI assistance: **suggestions, not verdicts.** Score suggestions are grounded in stated product context and are always the user's to override; the Strategy Advisor is generated separately from the math. AI sits inside the user's process as a thinking partner, not a replacement for it.

Access follows the same "low friction, real when you need it" logic: a guest can score a backlog immediately with nothing saved server-side (localStorage), or sign in (Clerk) to persist and sync work across sessions and devices (Supabase).

## Reflections

- **Normalizing to 1–100 was the right call for comparability, but it has a cost.** RICE's native units carry meaning a slider hides — "three person-months" is more legible than "Effort: 60." A future pass could let a team toggle native units back on per dimension.
- **Score suggestions need a visible confidence signal.** A suggested value the user can't distinguish from a hand-entered one re-creates the overconfidence problem RICE is meant to fight; surfacing *why* the model proposed a number is the next step.
- **A retrospective loop is still the missing piece.** Feeding actual outcomes back in six months later — and recalibrating the team's typical Confidence ratings against reality — remains the most valuable follow-on.

## Closing observation

The principle that proved most useful: **prioritization tools should make the team's reasoning legible, not replace it.** An AI that scores for you is faster than the alternative; an AI that suggests a score, shows the trade-off, and leaves the call to you produces decisions that survive the meeting after the workshop.
