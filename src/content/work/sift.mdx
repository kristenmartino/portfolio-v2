## Overview

**Read the news with civic footnotes.** Sift turns news into something you can actually understand — every politician, organization, bill, and political term in an article links to a plain-English explainer. The product earns its place not as an AI-curated alternative to wire feeds, but as a civic-literacy layer that decodes who the players are, what the bills do, and where each outlet's framing sits relative to the others.

## The hypothesis I started with — and the one that survived

I started building an AI-curated alternative to wire-feed aggregators: a hundred-plus sources, semantic clustering, side-by-side comparison of how outlets covered the same event. The pipeline worked. The frontend shipped. But the design that emerged from real reading sessions pointed at a different bottleneck.

Most readers do not lack volume. They lack context. They can read five outlets on the same Senate vote and still have no idea who the senator is, what the bill actually does, what the relevant lobbying body wants, or how the framing differs from the last time the question came up. The thing they actually need is not more articles — it is the political, organizational, and legislative scaffolding embedded around each story.

That is the version of Sift that shipped. Same engineering substrate, different unit of value: for people who want to follow what is happening without already knowing the players.

## What's there now

Sift is a news reader where every entity in a story — politician, organization, bill, outlet, civic term — links to a plain-English explainer:

- **Dossiers.** Each politician, organization, bill, and outlet has a structured page: who they are, what they have done, the relevant numbers. Citations are inline; the underlying source is always one click away.
- **Inline glossary.** Civic terms surface contextually inside the article itself — not in a separate panel a reader has to seek out. Defined where the reader needs them, in the language they need.
- **Adaptive primers.** When a story sits on top of complex policy (the Inflation Reduction Act, debt-ceiling mechanics, FTC consent decrees), the primer expands to fit what the reader needs without overwhelming what they do not.
- **Cross-spectrum framing.** When multiple outlets cover the same story, Sift shows what each chose to emphasize — described, not labeled "biased" or "objective." The reader does the interpretation; the product does the legwork.

## Pipeline

Early on the AI work happened at click-time, and the experience cost roughly fifteen seconds per category load. So the architecture inverted: a scheduled pipeline does the analytical work and the frontend serves enriched content from cache. Live latency went from 15s to ~50ms, and AI cost moved from a per-click tax to a budget I can throttle.

The pipeline (FastAPI + LangGraph + Anthropic) runs ten services: primer generation, entity extraction, entity linking to dossiers, summarization, story synthesis, story clustering, civic context generation, multi-source comparison, batched API client, and usage tracking. The frontend reads from Postgres.

## Implementation considerations

**Source curation became a quieter problem than I expected.** My initial corpus pulled from mainstream outlets via aggregated feeds. That corpus is fine for civic decoding — dossiers, glossary, and primers work the same regardless of source — but it neutralized the more aggressive features I had built (trust scoring, propaganda tagging, extremism flags). Mainstream outlets do not give those features anything to do. I left them out of the shipped surface rather than apply judgment claims I could not defend at the corpus level.

**The dossier graph proved more interesting than the cluster graph.** Cluster-based comparison was the original product unit — group articles about the same event, show the side-by-sides. What proved more useful was the entity graph: every politician, organization, and bill becomes a node, every story becomes a path, and a reader following any story gets pulled into the surrounding civic structure. The cluster surface still exists. The dossier surface is what gets daily use.

## Reflections

- **Per-paragraph primer triggers.** Primers attach to articles by topic match. A reader-pacing model that surfaces primers in the reading flow, not above the fold, is the next move.
- **Outlet observation, not outlet judgment.** Cross-spectrum compare shows side-by-side framing. The version worth shipping next describes what *each* outlet chose to emphasize, without claiming any of them are wrong. Observation is honest; labeling is brittle.
- **A trust layer for the corpus it was built for.** The flags I built (trust scoring, propaganda tagging, extremism) produce nothing meaningful applied to mainstream sources. The genuinely useful version of that layer would apply to propaganda outlets — a different product on a different corpus with a different audience.

## Closing observation

I started building an AI-curated alternative to wire-feed aggregators. The differentiator turned out not to be the AI — it was civic decoding. Sift is not "news synthesized by AI." It is news for people who want to follow what is happening without already knowing the players: *read the headline, learn the civics as you go.*

The AI is still load-bearing — primers, dossiers, and cross-spectrum framing all run on it. But the AI is infrastructure now, not the product. The product is the reader being able to follow a story without first having to research who everyone is.
